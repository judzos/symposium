ᴅᴏɴ †

Self-improvement essayist publishing on Substack. Direct address, building each piece from a named mechanism — a cognitive bias, a neurochemical loop, a Japanese or Stoic philosophy, a figure from myth or history — toward a concrete practice. The recurring argument: potential is lost by default through consumption, excuses and inherited belief, and recovered deliberately through creation, incremental progress, and a curated internal world.

Website: https://nqdonzo.substack.com

What they write about

16 recurring subjects across the 30 sources, each with the pieces that carry it.

Attention, consumption and the modern environment

Short-form video read as a variable-ratio reward schedule — the slot-machine comparison is explicit — plus the dopamine-spike to cortisol chain it runs, the attention economy stated as arithmetic (view → engagement → ad revenue), akrasia as the resulting loss of self-control, and the evolutionary-mismatch argument that hardware calibrated for scarcity is being fed abundance. Returns as doomscrolling in the habit lists, as Naval’s “the modern devil is cheap dopamine”, and as notifications stealing momentum.

In: The Consumption Dilemma · Remove These Five Destructive Habits That Are Destroying Your Potential · How to Destroy Your Potential in 90 Days · I’ll Make You Believe You Can Achieve Anything after Reading This · Read this If You Want to Cure Your Procrastination

Execution — starting, finishing, prioritising

Clarity and confidence as outputs of action rather than prerequisites for it (“the cold water won’t get any warmer if you jump late”), analysis paralysis, and procrastination given a cognitive-load account rather than a moral one: the brain’s energy budget, conscious bandwidth, state-dependent memory, the Zeigarnik effect, second-order consequence chains, and the outright denial of multitasking. The allocation half is the Pareto Principle with its provenance — Pareto 1906, Juran’s “vital few and trivial many” — used mainly in reverse, to name busywork.

In: The Execution Dilemma · Read this If You Want to Cure Your Procrastination · You Need a Mindset Shift on Priorities · Five Habits You Need to Implement Immediately

Excuses, inherited belief and identity

The recurring mechanism: an excuse is a neural pathway being myelinated until avoidance fires before conscious thought, and beliefs installed in childhood run as subconscious programs that awareness alone will not remove — evidence over months does. Named endpoints are learned helplessness, victim mentality and identity-based coping (“that’s just how things go for me”); the named antidote is an internal locus of control. Identity is treated as upstream of behaviour rather than downstream.

In: Discard These Excuses Immediately Before You Severely Cap Your Potential · Five Belief Systems You Need to Remove to Become Greater · Remove These Five Destructive Habits That Are Destroying Your Potential · How to Destroy Your Potential in 90 Days · I’ll Make You Believe You Can Achieve Anything after Reading This

Mental models and cognitive biases

Munger’s latticework — big ideas from the big disciplines, the man-with-a-hammer tendency, inversion, circle of competence, the map that is not the territory, the Lollapalooza effect, ignorance removal, second-order thinking — and the five biases marked for removal by the same two-step route: novelty bias and shiny object syndrome, loss aversion (worked through 2008), herd mentality and FOMO, Dunning–Kruger including its expert’s-doubt half, and overconfidence.

In: Why You Should Adopt Many Mental Models For Your Life · Rid Yourself of These Cognitive Biases so You Can Conquer · You Need a Mindset Shift on Priorities · How to Stop Wasting Your Life (and your time)

Compounding and incremental progress

Kaizen lifted out of manufacturing and applied to a person: small continuous steps, floors rather than ceilings, the bamboo’s invisible root years, Lucretius’ water wearing stone, 1% daily compounding to 37.7×, systems over goals, and the author’s own term “escape velocity” for the point where accumulated work converts into visible results. Hormozi’s “volume negates luck” is argued here and then qualified elsewhere — ten thousand kicks are worthless if they are subpar.

In: Kaizen: A Philosophy Every Creative Should Adopt · The Anatomy of Greatness: The Psychology of Being the Best · Five Crucial Lessons from the Human Embodiment of Self-Actualization: Bruce Lee · Nine More Timeless Lessons from Naval to Make You Greater

Greatness and what it costs

Greatness decomposed into traits — strong self-concept routed through ikigai, internal locus of control, intrinsic motivation, high agency, assiduity, delayed gratification tested by the marshmallow study — and then priced: trade-offs stated as non-optional, Kobe’s 3:30 AM, Jordan’s doubled work-rate becoming the team’s baseline, ego as the ceiling via Genghis Khan’s meritocracy. Why most people stop is answered statistically, with regression to the mean, and neurologically, with the path of least resistance myelinating until quitting is the identity. Environment, social circle (no yes-men), solitude as distinct from isolation, and Taleb’s antifragility are named as the inputs you actually control.

In: The Anatomy of Greatness: The Psychology of Being the Best · How You Can Reach Excellence in a World that Demands Lesser · How to Develop an Iron Mindset to Dominate · How to Develop Unwavering Confidence and Self-Belief

Burnout, recovery and the limits of optimisation

The counterweight to the rest of the corpus’s intensity: “are we chasing the vision, or the validation?”, the cortisol feedback loop of goal-attachment, monk mode reported as personal failure and busywork, Naval’s work-like-a-lion against grazing, rest between sets as what permits intensity, what neglected sleep costs through the glymphatic system, the law of diminishing returns against twelve-hour days, and a refusal to sacrifice your twenties for your thirties.

In: Forget “Locking In.” Sometimes You Need to Lock Out. · Five Belief Systems You Need to Remove to Become Greater · Kaizen: A Philosophy Every Creative Should Adopt

Habits, protocols and the neuroscience underneath

Five habits given with mechanisms — fasted morning runs (amygdala down, cortisol down, pain tolerance up), cold showers as a graduated Wim Hof protocol whose real yield is sitting with discomfort, journaling as “releasing internal tension by way of an external channel”, finishing what you start against a RAM-limited mind, and complimenting people, costed out in dopamine, oxytocin, serotonin and reward circuitry — against five to subtract: doomscrolling, broken promises to yourself, people-pleasing, vices, negative self-talk. Neuroplasticity and myelination are the engine in both directions, with ninety days as the point of permanence.

In: Five Habits You Need to Implement Immediately · Remove These Five Destructive Habits That Are Destroying Your Potential · How to Destroy Your Potential in 90 Days

Stoicism, non-attachment and the dichotomy of control

Marcus Aurelius through Meditations — the dichotomy of control, thoughts as the quality of life, the best revenge being not to be like your enemy, embodying rather than debating a philosophy, gratitude on waking — plus the distinction that carries a whole piece: detachment pushes a thought away and rehearses it (the pink elephant), non-attachment releases the claim on an outcome. Also Seneca and Epictetus’ premeditatio malorum, memento mori as a brake rather than a spur, and five Latin phrases read as portable dispositions.

In: How To Curate An Internal World So Unshakeable that Nothing Can Stop You · The Art of Non-Attachment (in Philosophy) · The Beauty of Latin Phrases: Five Powerful Sayings from Conquerors · How to Stop Wasting Your Life (and your time) · Modern Self-Help Books Isn’t Enough: Read History, Fiction, and Mythology

History, myth and the case against modern self-help

The industry sells shovels rather than gold, so read history, fiction and mythology instead — advice that survived harder conditions than ours. The recurring cast: Caesar weeping at Alexander’s statue, Diogenes telling Alexander to get out of his sunlight, Columbus anchoring his own status with an impossible ask, Hannibal crossing the Alps, Napoleon’s speed and then his Russia, Genghis Khan absorbing what he conquered, and Icarus read twice — flight worth dying for, and the pride that spent the altitude.

In: Modern Self-Help Books Isn’t Enough: Read History, Fiction, and Mythology · What You Can Learn from Icarus on Ambition · The Beauty of Latin Phrases: Five Powerful Sayings from Conquerors · The Anatomy of Greatness: The Psychology of Being the Best

Naval Ravikant’s framework

Eighteen lessons across two pieces: long-term games with long-term people, specific knowledge that cannot be taught or automated, desire as the fundamental delusion and happiness as the absence of wanting, the modern struggle as evolutionary mismatch, “save yourself” accountability, impatience with actions and patience with results, life lived in the arena rather than in theory — then inspiration as perishable, hard choices for an easy life, the optimistic contrarian, intelligence measured as getting what you want out of life, work that is play to you and work to others, and happiness as a trainable skill.

In: Nine Timeless Lessons from Naval To Make You Greater · Nine More Timeless Lessons from Naval to Make You Greater

Faith, self-belief and its calibration

Belief acted on before the evidence exists — Topuria’s faith placing the outcome outside himself, stated intention preceding the result, first-principles mastery of each discipline separately, Neville Goddard’s law of assumption qualified so it stays non-mystical, Muhammad Ali before Foreman — and the corpus’s own correction, since the same author later uses Topuria’s loss to Gaethje as the worked example of overconfidence bias. The tension between delusional optimism and staying close to objective reality is one the author names and does not resolve.

In: How to Develop Unwavering Confidence and Self-Belief · I’ll Make You Believe You Can Achieve Anything after Reading This · Rid Yourself of These Cognitive Biases so You Can Conquer · Remove These Five Destructive Habits That Are Destroying Your Potential

Other people — approval, boundaries and the circle

People-pleasing named as the single most destructive habit, boundaries as the highest form of self-respect, Aesop on pleasing all and Lao Tzu on being their prisoner, the ship that does not open holes to let water in. On the other side: an inner circle that tells uncomfortable truths instead of comforting lies, courage redefined for a digital era as indifference to judgment, authenticity traded for approval as the disease of the feed, and complimenting people against a norm that calls it glazing.

In: How to Destroy Your Potential in 90 Days · Remove These Five Destructive Habits That Are Destroying Your Potential · Five Belief Systems You Need to Remove to Become Greater · How You Can Reach Excellence in a World that Demands Lesser · Nine More Timeless Lessons from Naval to Make You Greater · The Consumption Dilemma

Meaning, purpose and finite time

Nietzsche’s why that makes any how bearable, Maslow’s self-actualization reached in Bruce Lee, the good life as a process rather than a state, and the paradox that meaning is generated by contrast — mapped to yin and yang, argued through cheat codes and frictionless goals. The urgency machinery is explicit: a vision and an anti-vision run together (the rat, the cheese, the cat), roughly 75 of each season, 109 billion people already dead, and time as the one commodity no amount of money buys back.

In: The Art of Perseverance · The Paradox of Life · Five Crucial Lessons from the Human Embodiment of Self-Actualization: Bruce Lee · How to Stop Wasting Your Life (and your time) · What You Can Learn from Icarus on Ambition

Neurodegeneration and protecting the brain

The longest piece in the corpus reads Everywhere at the End of Time against the actual pathology of Alzheimer’s — amyloid-beta plaques and tau tangles, hippocampal atrophy and ventricular expansion, retrogenesis, sundowning, aphasia decaying the album’s own cover titles, terminal lucidity in the final five minutes — and William Utermohlen’s self-portraits as the same decline in paint. The countermeasures are the point: sleep and glymphatic clearance, movement and BDNF, diet, and cognitive load heavy enough to keep synapses in use.

In: Everywhere at the End of Time: The Scariest Album Ever Created

The Genius Series

A recurring format rather than a subject: one figure studied per instalment — Charlie Munger (I), Naval Ravikant (II), Ilia Topuria (III), Michael Jordan (IV), Marcus Aurelius (V), Bruce Lee (VI) — each read for a transferable system rather than a biography.
