Status, Influence, and Control: A Deep Dive into Human Hierarchies

Hierarchy is not something people opt into. It runs constantly, below the level
of decision, and the piece’s claim is that you are either navigating it
consciously or being played by someone who is.

The scene it argues from

A man in a tailored suit snaps his fingers at a barista and demands to be served
first. She does not flinch: “i’ll get to you when i’m done with this order.” The
expectation of compliance does not land, and he waits like everyone else — a
hierarchy failing to align with someone’s self-perception. Most people never think
about this consciously; they just feel it, as the unease when their authority is
challenged, the automatic deference given to certain people, the way some
individuals hold a room and others cannot get a word in.

Why the structure exists at all

Humans did not invent dominance hierarchies; every social species has a ranking
system, chickens included — pecking order is the literal origin of the phrase.
The reason given is economic: resources are finite and conflict is costly. If
every dispute over food, shelter or a mate required a fight, the group would be too
injured to survive, so a settled ranking resolves disputes without violence, the
lower-ranked individual simply submitting. Humans run the identical mechanism
dressed in language and social norms — you do not challenge your boss the way you
challenge your younger brother.

This is described as hardwired rather than learned. Babies as young as 10
months are said to understand dominance hierarchies, expecting larger individuals
to prevail over smaller ones and showing surprise when they do not. The brain
carries dedicated circuitry for tracking social rank, running in the background and
generating confidence or insecurity from your perceived position: high status
triggering dopamine and serotonin, low status triggering cortisol and
suppressing serotonin. That is the argument for why status matters to people who
insist it does not.

Power is perception first

The sharper claim is that hierarchies exist in perception before they exist in
reality. You are not responding to an objective ranking but to your brain’s read of
social signals — confident posture and space taken up coded as high status,
hunching and quiet speech as low — and your behaviour adjusts to the code
automatically, before any conscious decision. The mechanism is framed as
survival-grade pattern recognition, since high-status individuals have more
resources and more ability to help or harm you.

Its weakness is that a heuristic can be gamed. Status can be projected with zero
actual power behind it, which is offered as the explanation for why con artists
work; legitimate authority that is not projected goes unrespected in the same way.
The barista is read back through this lens — she held real positional power,
controlling access to the coffee, but what decided the exchange was the confidence
she projected, while the suit’s wealth signals carried no situational power and a
framing that violated the norm.

The fields it draws on

Power dynamics is treated as an umbrella over several disciplines: psychology for
the individual-level mechanisms, social psychology for conformity and obedience,
evolutionary psychology for why status is wanted at all, transactional analysis for
the roles played in an exchange. Sociology zooms out — sociometry maps who is
central and who is peripheral, and network position is itself power, since
whoever connects separate groups outranks whoever is isolated. Game theory models
each exchange as cooperation or defection under information asymmetry.
Negotiation is formalised power struggle, decided by who holds better
alternatives, information and framing — which is why walking away is strong, since
it signals options. History supplies the pattern that structures always shift.

Two ways up

Dominance works through force, intimidation and coercion: people comply
because they fear the consequences. It is effective short-term and structurally
unstable — the moment the ability to threaten disappears, so does the hierarchy,
which is why dictatorships require constant surveillance and violence.

Prestige works through competence, generosity and respect: people defer
because you hold skills they value or have helped them before, and it survives the
loss of formal authority. The best leaders are said to use prestige and the worst to
lean on dominance for want of competence behind the position. Most real hierarchies
mix the two — a boss may have genuine expertise and the power to fire you — so the
diagnostic question is which one they rely on.

What it looks like in a conversation

Four everyday tells are named. Interrupting signals that your words matter more
than theirs; high-status people interrupt low-status people constantly and the
reverse almost never happens, so the most-interrupted person in a group is its
lowest-ranked. Controlling the frame decides outcomes — accepting the terms of
“why did you do that?” and defending yourself is the submissive move; refusing the
question and naming what actually matters is the dominant one. Strategic
silence works because most people feel pressure to fill it, which is why
interrogators and negotiators use it: the discomfort is filled by whoever is lower
in the exchange, often with information they should have kept. Physical space
is read directly, and the claim is that changing your posture shifts the dynamic.

How it gets abused

Where someone controls a resource you need, compliance can be extracted by
threatening to withhold it. Employer–employee relations are called inherently
unequal on this basis, since you need the paycheck more than they need you
specifically, and financial dependence inside a relationship produces the same
imbalance. Abusive relationships run the mechanism deliberately — isolating someone
from friends, controlling the finances, removing alternatives — because more
dependency is more power, and more power is more room to abuse without
consequence. Cults are the same machinery at group scale: isolation, information
bubbles, and leaving made impractical.

Two tactics are named. Gaslighting — “that didn’t happen,” “you’re remembering
it wrong,” “you’re too sensitive” — is power through psychological
destabilisation: someone who cannot trust their own judgement becomes dependent on
yours. Weaponized incompetence is feigning inability so the task passes to
someone else, holding the imbalance in place with plausible deniability intact.

Operating inside it

Five moves close the piece. Assess your objective power first — what resources
you control, what skills others value, whether you have alternatives if the
relationship ends; this is framed as information rather than a verdict. Stop
signalling submission unnecessarily: excessive apologising, hedging (“i just
think maybe possibly”), avoided eye contact and a physically small posture invite
the treatment they advertise, and the correction is neutrality rather than
aggression. Build prestige, not just dominance, because power resting only on
threat is fragile by construction. Know when to challenge and when to submit —
fighting every dynamic is exhausting, so push back when boundaries are being
violated, and push back through leverage rather than emotion: if you do X, I will
do Y, where Y is something they do not want. Exit when the structure is
fundamentally toxic, which is only available if alternatives were maintained in
advance. The ability to walk away is called the ultimate form of power.

Hierarchies are not going away; flatten one and it reforms, and the author expects
them amplified as the wealth gap widens. The open question is not whether they
exist but whether they rest on competence and mutual benefit or on coercion — which
depends on the people inside them, making it a choice rather than a condition.
