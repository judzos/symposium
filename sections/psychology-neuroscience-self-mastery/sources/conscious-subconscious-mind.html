The Conscious And Subconscious Mind: Everything You Need To Know

The part of you that feels like you processes roughly four ten-thousandths of a
percent of what reaches your senses — so emotions, judgements and most decisions
arrive from a system you never consented to program, and the leverage is not
thinking harder but changing what that system runs.

The argument

Assembled from a named reading list — Joseph Murphy’s The Power of Your Subconscious
Mind, Kahneman’s Thinking, Fast and Slow, Tor Nørretranders’ The User Illusion,
Jung’s collected works on the unconscious, and Freud. The essay’s structure is to
establish the size asymmetry first, then work outward to what follows from it.

Key Concepts

  • The asymmetry, in numbers. The conscious mind holds about seven items
    (plus or minus two) and processes 40–50 bits per second, against a total
    sensory input of about 11 million — leaving conscious awareness with roughly
    0.0004% of what arrives. It is also slow, effortful, single-focus and
    metabolically expensive, burning glucose, which is offered as why hard thinking is
    physically tiring.

  • What the subconscious actually runs: heartbeat, breathing, digestion, immune
    function; every memory including the unrecallable ones; learned motor programs like
    walking, driving, typing and speaking; snap judgements from micro-expressions never
    consciously registered; and the filtering that decides what the conscious mind gets
    shown at all. Freud’s unconscious, Jung’s collective unconscious and modern
    implicit processing are treated as three descriptions of the same substrate.

  • The CEO analogy. Conscious mind as chief executive — big decisions, direction,
    novel problems, but only a few things at once; subconscious as the thousands of
    employees running operations. The loop: input arrives, the subconscious filters it
    against stored patterns and generates an emotional response, routine matches are
    handled automatically, and only novel or complex cases are escalated. Whatever the
    conscious mind then decides becomes a new pattern stored for automatic handling
    next time. That is learning — conscious struggle converting into subconscious
    automation, which is why driving starts exhausting and ends invisible. The
    conclusion drawn is that the goal is not to use the conscious mind more but to
    train the subconscious to run better programs.

  • Programs installed without permission. Emotional reactions are subconscious
    output rather than choice — the immediate anger at an insult, the fear at a spider,
    nostalgia at a smell. Their origin is childhood conditioning: parental relationship
    patterns absorbed and later replayed with partners; being shouted at for mistakes
    installing mistakes = danger and anxiety around imperfection; being praised for
    quietness installing expression = risk. The developmental window given is the
    third trimester to about age seven, when the brain sits in a theta state and
    absorbs indiscriminately — offered as why political views so often track whoever
    raised you. This is also why conscious knowledge does not dislodge them: the
    subconscious is older, faster and more myelinated, so the emotion has already
    flooded the system by the time the conscious mind objects. Knowing something
    intellectually while feeling otherwise     is two systems running on different
    information.

  • The Reticular Activating System as the bridge, and the piece’s most practical
    claim. A bundle of neurons at the brainstem decides what gets through the filter,
    and you program it with conscious focus: buy a car and suddenly see that model
    everywhere, because it was always there and previously discarded as irrelevant.
    Focus on problems and it surfaces problems; focus on solutions and it surfaces
    those. The author is careful to separate this from manifestation — the mechanism is
    filtering rather than causation, same reality, different filter.

  • Cognitive biases as subconscious heuristics — speed-and-pattern shortcuts that
    once aided survival and now produce systematic error. Five are worked:
    confirmation bias, why opposed partisans watch one event and see two; the
    availability heuristic, where ease of recall stands in for likelihood, so recent
    news about crashes makes flying feel riskier; anchoring, which is why the
    struck-through original price makes an overpriced sale price feel like a deal;
    sunk cost, keeping people in failing situations to avoid conceding a past
    decision; and fundamental attribution error, blaming others’ character and one’s
    own circumstances. Named as features rather than flaws, correctable only by
    effortful conscious attention — which is why they persist.

  • Reprogramming needs repetition and emotion, not understanding. A hundred books
    on confidence leave insecurity intact, because knowing is not rewriting. The
    channels given: repetition in the thousands rather than tens (the mechanism behind
    affirmations working when they do); strong emotion, which is why trauma encodes so
    deeply and why peak experiences can shift programming faster than years of talk;
    hypnosis, as direct access past the conscious filter; visualisation with sensory
    and emotional detail, since the subconscious does not sharply distinguish vivid
    imagination from experience, which is the basis of athletic mental rehearsal; and
    meditation, which creates observation space between stimulus and response.

  • Dreams as offline processing. The conscious mind shuts down while the
    subconscious sorts the day, consolidates memory and works through emotional
    content — offered as why unprocessed trauma recurs as nightmares, and why sleeping
    on it     works. Three interpretations are laid side by side without adjudication:
    Freud’s wish fulfilment, Jung’s symbolic messages, and the neuroscientific reading
    of largely random firing retrospectively narrativised. The problem-solving cases
    cited are Mendeleev on the periodic table, Kekulé on benzene, and Howe on the
    sewing machine.

  • The superconscious, explicitly flagged as theory rather than science — Jung’s
    collective unconscious as the account of why separated cultures generate similar
    myths and archetypes. The author declines to adjudicate, noting the experience is
    real even where the explanation is contested, and lists deep meditation, flow,
    psychedelics and crisis as the reported routes.

“your conscious focus programs your subconscious perception… same reality, different filter.”
