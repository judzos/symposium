Game Theory Will Change Your Life

Core Argument

Every human interaction is a strategic game with structure, incentives, payoffs, and equilibria. Most people play blind — cooperating when they should defect, defecting when they should cooperate, staying trapped in bad equilibria because they can’t see the game structure. Game theory makes the invisible visible.

The Seven Game Structures

1. Prisoner’s Dilemma

You and a partner are arrested, can’t communicate. Betray → walk free (partner gets 10 yrs). Both betray → 5 yrs each. Both silent → 1 yr each. Individual logic drives betrayal; collective logic demands silence. Result: both betray, both get 5 years (worse than mutual cooperation). The game where rational individual choices produce irrational collective outcomes.

2. Nash Equilibrium

A stable point where nobody wants to change their strategy given what others are doing — even if everyone would be better off if all changed simultaneously. Real example: a couple stuck in a medium city (neither career hub nor peaceful retreat) because neither wants to sacrifice their preference first. Recognizing you’re in a Nash Equilibrium is the first step to escaping it.

3. Zero-Sum vs. Positive-Sum

  • Zero-sum: Your gain = my loss (poker, sports, one promotion slot)
  • Positive-sum: Both can win (business partnerships, trade, relationships)
  • The common error: Treating positive-sum games as zero-sum — competing and hoarding when cooperation would grow the pie for everyone

4. Repeated vs. One-Shot Games

One-shot → incentive to defect. Repeated → incentive to cooperate. Tit-for-Tat: Cooperate first; then mirror what they did. Wins more repeated-game tournaments than complex strategies. Maps directly to life: help those who help you; don’t help those who burned you. Reputation is your most valuable asset in repeated-game environments.

5. Coordination Games

Everyone benefits from doing the same thing — it doesn’t matter which thing, just that everyone coordinates. QWERTY persists not because it’s best but because everyone’s locked into it. Why rational people keep doing obviously suboptimal things: changing alone is worse than staying coordinated on the bad option.

6. Signaling Games

You can’t directly prove quality about yourself, so you do something costly that only someone with that quality could sustain:

  • Expensive degrees → “I’m smart/conscientious enough to get through this”
  • Luxury goods → “I can afford to waste this much money”
  • Insane work hours → “I have the work ethic to sacrifice this”
    Signaling arms race: Once everyone signals, you signal just to maintain position. Absolute waste increases; relative positions stay the same.

7. Tragedy of the Commons

Shared resource: individually rational to maximize use, collectively rational to limit use. Without coordination, overuse destroys the resource. Examples: overfishing, office kitchens, open-source software, social media content quality. Fixing it requires: regulation, privatization, or community enforcement.

How to Apply Game Theory

  1. Identify the game structure before making a move — wrong game ID = wrong strategy
  2. Look for Nash Equilibria you’re stuck in — stuck relationship, safe mediocrity, comfortable dysfunction
  3. In repeated games, optimize long-term — reputation is everything
  4. Recognize wasteful signaling — know when you’re spending to signal, not to get
  5. Create positive-sum frames — “how can we both win?” unlocks solutions zero-sum thinking misses
  6. Understand incentives, not just people — bad behaviour often = fucked incentive structure, not bad person
  7. Change games when you can’t win the current one — new career, new platform, new relationship

Key Concepts

  • Game-Theory — scientific study of strategic decision-making with multiple players
  • game-theory-social — the social-dynamics reading of the same seven structures
  • Prisoner’s Dilemma — rational individual choices producing irrational collective outcomes
  • Nash-Equilibrium — stable but suboptimal; trapped by mutual best responses
  • Zero-Sum-vs-Positive-Sum — the most important distinction in strategic thinking
  • Tit-for-Tat-Strategy — cooperate first, then mirror; most robust repeated-game strategy
  • Tragedy of the Commons — shared resources destroyed by individually rational overuse
  • Signaling Games — costly signals as credibility proxies; signaling arms races
  • Coordination Games — suboptimal standards persist because unilateral switching is worse
  • Repeated vs One-Shot Games — changes everything about optimal strategy
  • Ultimatum Game — why deals collapse even when both sides benefit; fairness beats rationality
