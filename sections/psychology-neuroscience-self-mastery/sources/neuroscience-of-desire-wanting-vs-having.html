The Neuroscience of Desire (Why Wanting Feels Better Than Having)

Dopamine evolved to drive pursuit rather than produce satisfaction, so
anticipation reliably outpays arrival — and once that is the operating pattern,
a brain will unconsciously protect the chase, including by sabotaging the thing
it was chasing.

The argument

The opening case is a classmate who spent a semester engineering encounters with a
girl, learned her music, finally asked her out, dated her three weeks and lost
interest entirely — explaining afterwards that the chase was fun but actually
being with her was kind of boring. The author’s reading is that this was not
dishonesty or even simply bad character: the chase genuinely was more satisfying
than the relationship, and the pattern generalises well beyond romance.

Key Concepts

  • The monkey experiment, used to establish the mechanism. A green light
    precedes a dropped banana; after enough repetitions the dopamine spike migrates
    forward — it now fires at the light, before any reward, and fires harder than
    the eating does. Then the spike flattens, because the pattern became reliable and
    certainty is boring. The interesting turn is what restores it: one day the
    light comes and no banana falls, then later five drop at random. Once the outcome
    becomes unpredictable, the response goes to maximum every time the light appears.
    Named as the variable ratio reward schedule and called the most addictive
    pattern in neuroscience, with the conclusion that the addiction is not to the
    reward but to the anticipation of maybe receiving one — and that the less
    predictable it is, the stronger the pull.

  • Why the system is built this way. Dopamine rises while moving toward something
    and falls on obtaining it, because an organism made fully satisfied by achievement
    would stop moving and die. The brain therefore rewards pursuit over arrival. In
    modern conditions this produces chronic dissatisfaction: months of work, a day or
    two of satisfaction, then the search for the next thing. Promotions, relationships,
    purchases and achievements all follow it — the imagined version is fuelled by
    anticipation dopamine, and what actually arrives is weaker and shorter-lived, which
    is offered as why people who achieve enormous success so often report emptiness.

  • Anxiety as anticipation inverted. Anxiety is the same machinery running on
    negative futures — simulations of bad outcomes keeping the nervous system alert.
    The counterintuitive claim is that it becomes self-sustaining not because it feels
    good but because it is familiar and engaging, giving the brain something to do.
    People with chronic anxiety therefore disturb their own calm, finding something to
    worry about when things go well, because a system adapted to constant threat
    anticipation reads calm as wrong. Hence just relax fails as advice: the pattern
    has to be retrained, not instructed away.

  • Achievement as the comedown. The imagined moment is built up over months, and
    reality cannot compete with an idealised construction — but the sharper point is
    neurochemical: the spike already happened during pursuit, so arrival lands while
    the system is coming down. Achievement is the dopamine crash after the
    anticipation high.     This is why goal-oriented people struggle with satisfaction,
    hitting a milestone and immediately setting the next one, never present with what
    they have because attention has already moved.

  • Uncertainty is the active variable, not reward size. This is what makes
    gambling and infinite scroll work — you do not know which spin, refresh or
    notification carries the payoff, and the uncertainty itself produces the spike.
    Early dating runs on it: will they reply, do they want to see me again. Then a
    relationship resolves the uncertainty, and dopamine falls — not because the person
    became less interesting but because the anticipation ended. The essay draws the
    practical consequence that long-term relationships require deliberate effort
    against the brain’s preference for uncertain anticipation over predictable
    satisfaction.

  • Self-sabotage before achievement, explained rather than moralised. Stopping
    work just before graduating, picking a fight when a relationship is going well,
    making the decision that tanks a business as it takes off — irrational from
    outside, coherent from inside the dopamine system, because arrival ends the
    anticipation     that has been the primary reward source for however long the pursuit
    ran. Staying in pursuit keeps the supply going and avoids the post-achievement
    crash, and the risk that the next thing proves less engaging. Dopamine optimisation
    does not weigh long-term wellbeing; it maintains the pattern it adapted to.

  • The hedonic treadmill as the same regulation. Baseline returns regardless of
    circumstance — lottery wins spike then settle, catastrophic injury drops then
    recovers — because the system wants homeostasis rather than height. So a raise is
    the new normal within a week and the dream car within a month, while anticipation
    does not habituate as fast as possession    : the car can be fantasised about for
    months and adapted to in weeks.

  • The intensity paradox. What you want most intensely will tend to satisfy you
    least, because intense wanting builds the largest anticipation spike and reality
    cannot compete with it — so disappointment scales with the strength of the desire.
    Conversely, things stumbled into without buildup are frequently more satisfying,
    having no inflated expectation to compete with. The conclusion is not to stop
    wanting but to recognise that wanting is the drug and getting may be the
    comedown    .

What it prescribes

Practise presence with what you have — actually stopping at an achievement rather
than immediately setting the next target, which will feel uncomfortable and
gradually teaches the system that arrival can pay. Reduce unpredictable
reward-checking, since every phone check trains the brain that anticipation feels
good and makes arrival worse by comparison. Find satisfaction in process rather
than outcome, because process is renewable and outcome is not — the person who
loves writing has a dopamine source that never ends; the person who only loves
having written is always chasing. And notice the pattern in yourself without
judging it, on the grounds that awareness is what stops it operating unobserved.

“most people spend their entire lives in the gap between where they are and where they want to be.”
