You Are Aging At A Speed You’re Choosing: The Neurobiology of Aging

There is no single “your age” — dozens of clocks run at once inside you, each responding to different inputs — and roughly 80% of future health sits in the epigenetic layer rather than the genes, meaning DNA is largely not fate but the reading of it is.

Core Argument

The frame is the author’s grandmother, whose worn, veined hands she would hold out saying “this is where time lives,” while telling him eyes never age — that they stay the same from birth to death, and to fall in love with a woman’s eyes first “because everything else was going to change.” He watched her age: the hands got worse, the walk slowed, the eyes stayed exactly what they had always been. “She didn’t have a single biology class in her life and she’d stumbled onto the truest thing about aging I’ve ever heard. Different parts of you age at completely different speeds.”

The addition that took him years of reading: which clock runs fast and which runs slow is not fixed. Aging is not up for debate; the rate of it is “one of the most modifiable things about being alive.”

Key Concepts

  • Chronological versus biological age — the calendar number versus what the cells say, now measurable via the Horvath clock, which reads chemical marks on DNA. Some people are ten, fifteen or twenty years younger biologically than someone at the same calendar age, “and the gap between those two numbers is almost entirely written by how you’ve been living.” Your passport says 35 while the heart may run at 28, the immune system at 48, the metabolism “like someone who hasn’t stood up in twenty years.”
  • Growth and longevity pull in opposite directions — the counterintuitive centrepiece. The periods when you feel most alive and invincible — infancy, puberty — are exactly when you age fastest. Research direction cited: the slower you develop as a child and the later puberty arrives, the longer you tend to live; animals bred to grow fast and mature early die young, while those that take their time last. Growth hormone is pro-aging, not a fountain of youth — animals engineered with barely any (the small ones, the dwarfs) live longest by a wide margin. “The body can build or the body can maintain. It struggles to do both hard at the same time.” The author applies this to his own training: lifting heavy four to five times weekly to maintain muscle and hormonal environment rather than chase mass, explicitly ruling out steroids.
  • The epigenome as scratched reader — David Sinclair’s framing, presented as the model that reorganised the author’s thinking. DNA is the music (and it is vast — unwound, a single cell’s DNA stretches about six feet; all of it in your body would reach the moon and back eight times), folded with precision that determines which songs play in which cell. The epigenome is the reader deciding “in this cell, play the liver songs.” When young the reader is flawless. Aging is what happens when it gets scratched: genes that should stay silent in a brain cell switch on, genes that should play get shut off, and the cell begins losing its identity. The photocopy analogy carries it — copy a copy a thousand times deep and letters blur, edges soften. “That slow loss of information is what aging fundamentally is. Not wear and tear on parts but literal lost instructions.” Around 80% of future health and longevity comes down to this layer, not the genes themselves.
  • Telomeres and the cost of chronic stress — chromosomes as shoelaces, telomeres as the plastic tips that stop fraying, shortening with each division until the cell stops dividing or dies. Normal loss is roughly 30–35 base pairs per year. The study cited tracked 411 healthy adults over three years and found those with higher cortisol reactivity lost about 107 extra base pairs — “roughly two additional years of cellular aging. From stress. In three years.” The mechanism restated: “your body can’t tell the difference between real danger and the Sunday night email.”
  • System-by-system decline — the prefrontal cortex begins losing volume in the early 30s, with unused synaptic connections pruned, processing slowed and working memory narrowed; the protective factor is never ceasing to demand something genuinely new, since people who take up complex skills in their 40s and 50s and “stay intellectually uncomfortable on purpose” show better outcomes. “The brain responds to demand… stop demanding and it happily accommodates the lower standard you gave it.” Arterial walls stiffen (answered with zone 2 cardio four to five times weekly at conversational pace, for mitochondrial density and cardiac efficiency). Sarcopenia starts in the 30s and accelerates when permitted, and skeletal muscle directly governs metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and “whether you’ll still be able to get off the floor on your own at 80.” Gut microbial diversity narrows and the lining grows more permeable, leaking inflammation systemically and “speeding up aging in every other organ at once.”
  • Senescent cells — damaged cells that refuse to die and are not cleared, “not alive enough to do their job but not dead enough to get removed,” leaking inflammation into surrounding tissue. “They poison the neighborhood.” Linked to systemic inflammation and cancer. Excess iron is named as an accelerant, which prompts a broader caution: “the average recommendation and the optimal one for your body are not the same thing,” and blood work over time beats a single snapshot.
  • hsCRP as the untracked number — C-reactive protein as the best single inflammation marker, and inflammation as one of the loudest predictors of aging rate and lifespan. The trap identified: normal blood sugar and a clean bill of health while walking around with high CRP “that’s quietly predicting a heart attack years out.”
  • Hormesis — the good-stress distinction — brief cold exposure spikes norepinephrine and triggers a cellular stress response leaving the system tougher, the same logic as fasting and heavy training: “you’re giving the cell just enough of a threat to wake its defenses up, then letting it recover.” Set explicitly against chronic cortisol — “one is a sharp signal that ends. The other is a siren that never stops.”
  • The subjective age effect — people who genuinely feel younger than their calendar age show better health, slower cognitive decline and longer lives than same-age people who feel every year. The author is blunt about 28- and 31-year-olds describing their prime as behind them: “that’s learned helplessness pointed at your own body.” Most who fall apart in their late 20s and 30s “stopped asking anything of their bodies and started believing a story about what was supposed to happen to them. They swapped training for nostalgia and called it aging.” Chronic back pain at 30 is attributed to twelve hours of sitting rather than to years. “The mind writes the story and the body reads it back.”
  • Reversal is measured, not theoretical — a 2023 study using epigenetic clocks produced an average reduction of around three years of biological age in eight weeks through diet, sleep, movement, stress work and targeted supplementation. Larger reversals take longer, “but the direction is proven.” The protocol: seven to nine hours of sleep in a dark cold room (during which the brain “runs a literal wash cycle, flushing out the metabolic garbage”) — named the foundation everything else sits on; a 14–16 hour daily eating window to enable autophagy, with the honest warning that the first two or three weeks “suck” and should be staged in rather than attempted cold turkey; zone 2 cardio; maintenance strength training; deliberate hormetic stress; and chronic cortisol reduction.
