The Psychology of Loneliness

Being alone and being lonely are different things, and the piece’s argument is
that the people everyone worries about are usually the ones who understand the
difference — while the ones with full calendars are the ones hiding it.

Mike

The essay opens on a friend it calls Mike. He is invited out every weekend, reads
the group chat, and puts the phone face down. He is not depressed and does not have
social anxiety; he would simply rather be alone than spend four hours making small
talk with people he is only moderately interested in. His friends read this as
flaky or stuck up, and some have stopped inviting him. But when Mike does show
up, everyone knows it means something — no obligation hangouts, no “i guess i
should show my face” appearances. Most people spend time with whoever is available,
whoever invites them, whoever makes them feel less alone in the moment; Mike spends
it only on people he actually cares about, which makes him incredibly lonely and
incredibly loyal at the same time.

Validation-seeking, and what it is hiding

The fear driving the packed calendar is not being physically alone but being alone
in the existential sense — disconnected, unseen, forgotten. So people fill their
lives with any people, because being around others feels like proof they matter
and exist in a meaningful way. This is named social validation seeking, and the
charge against it is that it does not produce connection: these people aren’t
actually connected, they’re just occupied, surrounded by others and still lonely
because none of it is genuine.

The selective introvert is the contrast case, and comfort with solitude is what
makes the difference. Someone who does not need company to feel validated does not
fill a calendar to feel significant — so when they do choose to spend time with
you, it is deliberate. The choice, not the frequency, is what changes the
relationship.

Why the friendship is different in kind

A friendship offered by someone comfortable alone is not transactional: they
are not there for validation, entertainment, or distraction from their own
thoughts. Most friendships, by contrast, are built on convenience and proximity —
coworkers because you see them daily, neighbours because they are next door,
classmates because they are simply there. The piece does not condemn those, only
refuses to call them the same thing as being chosen.

The selective person declines until people stop inviting, and invests completely in
the few who remain, because emotional bandwidth is finite and undivided: two or
three people get the full attention that would otherwise be spread across dozens.
Those are the friends who remember the thing you mentioned weeks ago and check in
when you are going through something.

The author includes himself in the diagnosis, and does not soften it: he says maybe
to plans and cancels day-of — not because something better came up, but because he
does not want to go, and he would rather be honest and irritate people than show up
half-present. “I’d rather have 3 friends who actually know me than 30 who think
they do.”

Solitude as the source of substance

Introverts are assumed to be boring; the claim here is the reverse. Time alone is
what builds internal depth — reading, thinking, odd interests, research rabbit
holes, and opinions actually formed rather than repeated from whatever is trending.
People who are constantly out are described as living in reactive mode: responding
to others, performing for an audience, never sitting with a thought long enough to
develop it. The catch is that this depth is only visible to whoever gets let in,
which most people will not be.

Voluntary loneliness is reframed as high standards rather than a deficit. Time
and energy are finite, so spending them on people who do not enhance your life is
called a form of self-betrayal — better alone and bored than surrounded and
drained, better one meaningful three-hour conversation than ten shallow
twenty-minute ones. From outside this looks like loneliness; from inside it is
freedom from social obligation and from maintaining relationships you do not value.

The irony the piece presses: the person with 500 followers and a packed weekend is
probably lonelier than the one with twelve who has not posted in six months.
Everyone is lonely; they only hide it differently — the extrovert by never
being alone, the introvert by not hiding it at all. And it is the introvert
everyone worries about.

The honest counter-case

The essay then turns on its own argument, which is the part that keeps it from
being a defence of avoidance. Selective solitude can be a problem: there is a
difference between genuine comfort alone and using “I’m just selective” as cover
for avoiding vulnerability — fear of rejection, of being known, of the risk
connection requires. The author says he has been there and probably still is
sometimes.

The test offered is empirical rather than introspective. Genuinely selective
people have deep connections, just not many; people using selectivity as a shield
have none at all — one is healthy boundaries, the other is avoidant attachment
wearing a mask. Which you are is worth establishing, because pretending to be
content while actually being lonely is called a special kind of torture.

What loyalty is measured by

The closing definition: loyalty is not how often someone shows up but why.
Someone who hangs out with you for want of anything better leaves the moment
something better appears; someone who chooses you specifically stays through every
point where fading away would have been easier. For a person to whom solitude is
genuinely appealing, choosing you over it is the whole signal — “the ‘i actively
choose you over solitude’ kind” of loyalty rather than the guilt-trip of long
tenure.

So a friend like this should not be read by conventional metrics: no daily texts,
slow replies, cancelled plans — and present when it actually matters. And if you
are that person, the instruction is to stop apologising for it. In a world that
measures worth by follower counts and full calendars, choosing depth over breadth
is treated as a radical act, and the loneliness it looks like from outside is at
least the honest version.
