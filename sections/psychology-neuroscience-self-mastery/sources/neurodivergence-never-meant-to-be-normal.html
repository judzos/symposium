Neurodivergence: Why Your Brain Was Never Meant To Be Normal

Neuroplasticity says the brain can change; neurodiversity says brains do not all start from the same place — and “normal” is only the centre of a distribution, never a default template a brain is supposed to match.

Core Argument

The piece is framed as the necessary correction to Neuroplasticity. Knowing the brain rewires can leave the mistaken assumption that all brains begin at the same starting point. Not in subjective experience — values, personality, worldview, which everyone accepts differ — but in physical architecture: how neurons connect, the strength of those connections, neurotransmitter balance, and how information is filtered, processed and prioritised. Same ingredients (fat, water, protein, electrical signals), different machines. “Our brains are like fingerprints.”

Normal is a statistical average, not a correctness claim. The average adult height is not the correct height; the average resting heart rate is not the correct resting heart rate. Normal describes the centre of a distribution and “says nothing about what’s right.”

Key Concepts

  • What actually varies — five axes, each illustrated concretely. Sensory filtering: two people in the same café with identical noise and lighting, one for whom background stays background, one processing the scrape of a chair two tables over, the specific frequency of the espresso machine, the flicker in the overhead light — same environment, different calibration, and mostly not intentional. Sustained attention: one person holds a dense text for hours, another degrades after eight minutes. Reward prediction: dopamine pathways governing what feels rewarding vary measurably, so for some the anticipation of reward creates strong motivational pull while for others the same reward barely registers until immediate and concrete. Social cognition: reading tone and implicit cues are complex tasks performed with wildly different automatic ease. Executive function: planning, prioritising, task initiation and switching, time management.
  • Executive function is neurological before it is moral — the piece’s sharpest claim against the productivity framing. “The person who struggles to start tasks, who can’t maintain a schedule, who loses track of time constantly, might not be lazy” but running genuinely different executive-function software.
  • ADHD as an interest-based attention system, not a broken one — the central mechanism. A neurotypical brain can direct attention voluntarily regardless of stimulation: you decide it matters, so you focus. The ADHD brain directs attention toward whatever is genuinely interesting, novel, urgent or emotionally significant, and the prefrontal cortex’s ability to override that for something low-stimulation is significantly weaker. This explains the person who cannot focus on homework for fifteen minutes but sits in a gaming chair for ten hours — the game supplies immediate feedback loops, novel challenges, social stimulation and clear progress markers; homework supplies none. “If the reward isn’t salient enough, the attention doesn’t fire.”
  • The neuroplasticity paradox — the piece’s most striking observation, and the reason it says calling this a discipline problem is damaging. Rewiring requires focused sustained attention applied consistently over time, because that is how Myelination builds pathways. But if the attention system does not fire reliably for anything not immediately stimulating, “you’re trying to use the exact tool the condition has compromised to fix the condition itself. It’s like trying to fix a broken hand using only that hand.” The system you must engage to change is the system hardest for you to engage.
  • Institutions encode one cognitive profile — the ideal student sits still for extended periods, sustains voluntary attention regardless of interest, follows sequential instructions, switches tasks on command, and tolerates constant ambient noise and interruption. None of those requirements are neutral; each advantages particular neurological profiles. The piece notes mass public education “was literally designed to teach people to be good workers and follow the rules.” Einstein’s fish is extended into a full test: give a monkey, fish, horse, elephant and penguin the same tree-climbing exam and grade them on it — the fish is not failing at intelligence but at a task built for a different body. “School was the tree.”
  • The invisible cost of workarounds — the author scored 80–90 in school but by self-study, reading independently, tracing problems to first principles and reading lecture slides at home, having “clocked out about ten minutes into most lectures.” He is careful about the framing: “I’m not slower because of it but the system wasn’t built for it.” Many neurodivergent people are paying that workaround energy on top of the work itself, and it is invisible to observers.
  • Advantages stated without inflation — the piece explicitly refuses the superpower framing as “usually cringe and oversimplified”: pattern detection across complex data (the attention system notices connections and anomalies others filter out), extraordinary depth of focus when the subject genuinely engages, solutions arrived at by unconventional routes (“the box isn’t even in their field of view”), domain expertise from intense interest patterns common in autism, and striking memory for categories most brains discard. The honest closing on this: these exist alongside real challenges, and the point is that “the same neurological differences that create difficulty in one environment can create advantage in another.”

Notable Passages

“If you judge a fish by its ability to climb a tree, it will live its whole life believing that it is stupid.” — Albert Einstein

“Normal is a statistical centre. Never mistake it for a default template.”

“The person who seems distracted may be noticing things you literally do not notice. Picking up signals that your brain has filtered before they reached your awareness.”
