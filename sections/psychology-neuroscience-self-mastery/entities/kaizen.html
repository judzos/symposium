Kaizen (改善)

Continuous improvement. The cited essays operationalize kaizen as making a change small enough to repeat, often expressed as “1% better every day.” The percentage is a metaphorical planning unit, not a measurement of human improvement.

flowchart LR
  S[Start absurdly small — one page, not ten] --> W1[Week 1 — show up, the outcome is not the goal]
  W1 --> R[Repetition myelinates the pathway, at its own pace]
  R --> I[Increment by one unit each week]
  I --> R
  R --> C[1.01 to the 365 — roughly 37 times over]
  B[Start at ten] --> BO[Burned out by week 2]
  BO --> Z[1.00 to the 365 — exactly 1]

Core Principle

The arithmetic 1.01^365 ≈ 37.78 shows what happens when a quantity literally compounds by 1% each period. The sources use it to dramatize consistency; skills and habits are multidimensional, include plateaus and feedback, and do not increase by a fixed percentage each day.

Neuroscience Mechanism

The sources connect repetition with plasticity and myelination: rehearsed actions can become easier and more automatic. That supports regular practice, but does not establish that “the pathway becomes the identity,” that all learning is limited by myelination, or that repetition without feedback guarantees improvement.

Why does the neuroscience favour small daily change over an intense short push?

Repetition across time can strengthen and automate a practiced pathway. The sources use myelination to explain that advantage; they do not show that a burst never helps or that myelination is the only mechanism involved.

Practical Application

  • Start absurdly small (one page/day reading, not ten)
  • Week 1 goal: build the habit of showing up, not achieving the outcome
  • Increment by 1 unit each week
  • The source’s week-12 comparison illustrates how a sustainable small routine can outlast an unsustainable large one; it is not a guaranteed timetable

What is the week-one goal in a kaizen protocol?

Showing up, not the outcome. The habit is what is being installed; performance is deferred until there is something for it to run on.

The fitness equivalent is progressive overload: gradually increase the stimulus as the current load becomes manageable.

Key Insight

“We massively overestimate how fast we can change and massively underestimate what consistent small change produces over time.” — ixcarus

Which two estimation errors does kaizen correct?

Overestimating how fast change can happen, and underestimating what small consistent change produces over time. Both push toward the intense short push that fails.
