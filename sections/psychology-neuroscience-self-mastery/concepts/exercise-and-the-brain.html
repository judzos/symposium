Exercise and the Brain

The cited essays present exercise as a broadly supported input to brain health and use BDNF, vascular change, stress regulation, sleep, and metabolic effects to explain why. BDNF is one pathway rather than the sole reason, and “more than almost anything else” is source rhetoric rather than a head-to-head comparison across interventions.

BDNF: Miracle-Gro for the Brain

Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) is a protein that promotes the growth, maintenance, and survival of neurons. John Ratey, the Harvard psychiatrist who wrote “Spark: The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the Brain,” called BDNF “Miracle-Gro for the brain.”

Exercise — specifically aerobic exercise — is the most reliably powerful stimulus for BDNF release. The mechanism: sustained aerobic effort signals to the brain that the organism is in a state of physical challenge requiring enhanced cognitive performance. The evolutionary logic: in the ancestral environment, physical exertion was usually associated with either pursuing prey or escaping threat — both situations requiring peak cognitive function.

What is the evolutionary logic linking aerobic effort to BDNF release?

Sustained exertion signalled either pursuit or escape, and both demanded peak cognition. Physical challenge is therefore read as a cue to upgrade the brain rather than as a drain on it.

BDNF’s effects:

  • Promotes neurogenesis (growth of new neurons, primarily in the hippocampus)
  • Enhances synaptic plasticity (strengthens existing connections; makes new ones easier to form)
  • Supports long-term potentiation (the cellular mechanism of memory formation)
  • Counteracts cortisol’s neurotoxic effects (BDNF protects neurons from stress-induced damage)
  • Elevates mood and reduces anxiety through mechanisms beyond serotonin

The hippocampus — the brain region most critical for memory and learning — shows demonstrable volume increases in people who exercise regularly. The inverse: a sedentary lifestyle is associated with hippocampal atrophy.

Neurogenesis: New Brain Cells in Adults

The sources present adult hippocampal neurogenesis as the reversal of an older dogma and connect it to behaviour. The extent and functional significance in adult humans are not established by these essays as cleanly as the word “definitively” suggests.

Enhances neurogenesis: Aerobic exercise (the most powerful single stimulus), caloric restriction/intermittent fasting, environmental enrichment (novelty, learning), stress management, adequate sleep.

Suppresses neurogenesis: Chronic stress and elevated cortisol (potently), sleep deprivation, social isolation, sedentary behavior, chronic alcohol use.

The relevance: neurogenesis is not merely about brain cell count. New neurons in the hippocampus are critical for pattern separation (distinguishing similar memories) and cognitive flexibility (applying learning to new situations). The brains with active neurogenesis are literally more capable of nuanced cognition than brains with suppressed neurogenesis.

Why does hippocampal neurogenesis matter beyond raw neuron count?

New hippocampal neurons carry pattern separation — telling similar memories apart — and cognitive flexibility. Suppressed neurogenesis costs the nuance, not just the number.

Demargination: Cold Exposure and Immune Function

A specific mechanism relevant to both exercise and cold exposure: demargination.

White blood cells that are “marginated”—resting against blood-vessel walls—can be temporarily recruited into circulation by physical stress. A higher circulating count reflects redistribution; it is not by itself proof of better immunity or improved resistance to infection.

Cold exposure (cold showers, ice baths) produces particularly potent demargination along with a spike in norepinephrine (up to 300% increase after 20-second cold water immersion at 14°C) — which has downstream benefits for mood, attention, and metabolic rate.

What is demargination?

The recruitment of white blood cells resting against vessel walls into active circulation, triggered by physical stress such as intense exercise or cold immersion — raising circulating immune cell counts for hours afterwards.

Anandamide: The Runner’s High

The “runner’s high” has long been attributed to endorphins. More recent research suggests anandamide — the brain’s endocannabinoid — is the primary neurochemical responsible for the euphoria and the creative connectedness that follows extended aerobic exercise.

Anandamide:

  • Is lipid-soluble, unlike endorphins, and therefore crosses the blood-brain barrier readily
  • Produces mild euphoria, reduced anxiety, and heightened sensory pleasure
  • Promotes lateral thinking and cross-domain pattern recognition (the same neurochemical present in flow state)
  • Has antidepressant effects through mechanisms independent of serotonin

This is why a good run often produces the insight that sitting at the desk for two hours failed to generate. It is not coincidence or rest — it is specific neurochemical action.

What is now credited with the runner's high, and why does a run produce insight?

Anandamide rather than endorphins. It crosses the blood-brain barrier readily and promotes lateral thinking and cross-domain pattern recognition — the same endocannabinoid present in flow.

Activity ranges reported by the sources

The essays report the following general activity ranges. Appropriate intensity and dose depend on health, disability, training status, medication, and recovery; the list is not an individualized exercise prescription:

  • 150-300 minutes per week of moderate aerobic exercise shows significant cognitive benefits
  • 3-5 sessions per week produces BDNF effects; single sessions provide acute benefits that last hours
  • Intensity matters: Zone 2 cardio (conversational pace) over duration provides maximum BDNF response; high-intensity interval training adds different benefits (norepinephrine, metabolic efficiency)
  • Resistance training contributes to cognitive benefits through IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor) and different pathways from aerobic exercise; optimal protocol includes both

The morning timing advantage: exercise before cognitively demanding work produces elevated BDNF, norepinephrine, and dopamine in the brain during the subsequent work session. The brain is literally in a higher-performance state for 2-4 hours post-exercise.

Why place demanding cognitive work after exercise rather than before?

BDNF, norepinephrine and dopamine stay elevated for two to four hours afterwards. The session is being run on a brain in a measurably higher-performance state.
