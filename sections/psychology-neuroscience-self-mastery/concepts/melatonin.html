Melatonin

The cited essays frame melatonin primarily as a darkness and circadian-timing signal rather than a conventional sedative. It can influence sleep propensity and timing, so “does not cause sleep” is a corrective simplification rather than a complete pharmacological account.

The Two Melatonins

Most people know melatonin as the sleep hormone. The cited essays distinguish two biologically distinct forms with different contexts, functions, and concentrations.

Pineal melatonin (circadian signaling melatonin) comes from the pineal gland deep in the brain. The gland secretes it into the bloodstream as light diminishes. It then serves as the body’s darkness signal, telling every organ system that it is nighttime and triggering the hormonal conditions permissive to sleep.

Intracellular melatonin (mitochondrial melatonin) is produced inside cells, in the mitochondria, in response to infrared light exposure. It remains local and acts as a powerful mitochondrial antioxidant rather than entering the bloodstream. Its concentrations can be up to 20 times higher than anything the pineal gland releases. This form is covered in the enhance-immune-system concept (sunlight and mitochondrial health).

What are the two biologically distinct melatonins?

Pineal melatonin, secreted into the bloodstream as the body’s darkness signal, and intracellular melatonin, produced inside mitochondria in response to infrared light. The second never enters the blood and acts locally as an antioxidant.

Production: The Darkness Signal

Light input to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) controls melatonin’s precise circadian rhythm:

  1. During daylight hours, light signals to the SCN suppress pineal melatonin production
  2. As light diminishes in the evening, SCN suppression lifts
  3. The pineal gland begins melatonin secretion — typically starting 2–3 hours before sleep onset in adults with healthy circadian timing
  4. Melatonin rises through the night, peaking in the early morning hours (2–4 AM)
  5. As morning light arrives, melatonin production is suppressed and secretion ends

The morning light exposure that anchors the circadian clock each day sets this timing. Early morning light schedules earlier melatonin onset and makes earlier sleep onset more reliable. Delaying or skipping morning light starts the countdown later, shifts melatonin onset, and leaves you physiologically unready for sleep at your intended bedtime.

Light Suppression: The Modern Problem

The same short-wavelength light (blue/white, roughly 446–484 nm) that anchors the circadian clock in the morning suppresses melatonin at night. The mechanism runs through the same photoreceptors (ipRGCs) and SCN pathway.

Screens such as phones, tablets, laptops, and televisions emit substantial blue-spectrum light. Research cited by the source consistently shows that 2 hours of screen use before bed delays melatonin onset by 1–1.5 hours and reduces peak concentrations by 20–30%. Across a week, the circadian clock undergoes a meaningful delay, sleep onset becomes difficult, and sleep duration shrinks as morning obligations pull wake time forward before the delayed sleep period can finish.

Evening blue-light-blocking glasses filter the 400–500 nm wavelengths that activate the ipRGC pathway while allowing other light through, which prevents this melatonin suppression.

Melatonin and Sleep Architecture

The essays describe melatonin as a timing hormone rather than a sedative. The presence of melatonin in the bloodstream does not cause unconsciousness. It signals nighttime to the body’s systems, which:

  • Lowers core body temperature (a prerequisite for sleep onset)
  • Shifts the autonomic nervous system toward parasympathetic dominance
  • Reduces cortisol (melatonin and cortisol are inversely regulated)
  • Creates the hormonal permissiveness for the brain to transition into sleep stages

People who produce insufficient melatonin because of chronic evening light exposure, aging, or high stress have a timing-signal deficit rather than a sedation deficit. The sleep initiation pathway cannot begin its descent until the body receives the “nighttime” signal.

If melatonin is not a sedative, what does it actually do?

It signals darkness. Its presence lowers core temperature, shifts the autonomic system toward parasympathetic dominance and suppresses cortisol — creating the conditions under which sleep can begin, rather than causing it.

Supplementation claims and their boundary

The sources argue that supplement marketing and common doses are poorly matched to melatonin’s timing role. This wiki records that critique but does not prescribe a dose: product content varies, indications differ, and age, pregnancy, medication, health conditions, and timing all matter.

The clinical evidence:

  • Meta-analysis of melatonin in healthy adults: average increase in total sleep time of 3.9 minutes
  • Effect on sleep onset latency in healthy users: minimal, inconsistent

Commercial melatonin supplements come in doses of 1mg, 3mg, 5mg, and 10mg, while the body’s natural peak production is approximately 0.1–0.3 mg. Supplements on shelves contain 10–20× the physiological dose because companies compete on quantity (consumers associate higher doses with stronger effects) rather than efficacy research.

Why might a supraphysiological melatonin dose make things worse over time?

It can desensitise melatonin receptors and suppress the body’s own production through negative feedback — so the supplement gradually creates the deficit it was taken to correct.

Supraphysiological doses of melatonin do not produce proportionally greater benefit. Over time, they may desensitize melatonin receptors and suppress the body’s own production through negative feedback, creating dependence on supplementation.

The population for whom melatonin has legitimate utility:

  • People with true insomnia, where natural melatonin production is genuinely reduced
  • Shift workers attempting to reset circadian timing
  • Individuals managing jet lag across multiple time zones
  • Older adults, in whom natural melatonin production declines with age

For people whose delayed sleep timing comes from behavioral causes such as evening light exposure, irregular schedules, or late caffeine, supplemental melatonin addresses a symptom while the cause continues.

The source advocates lower doses and earlier timing than many commercial products. Individualized instructions require an assessment of the actual indication and relevant clinical guidance.

Melatonin and Immune Function

Pineal melatonin has additional functions beyond sleep timing. It acts as a direct immunomodulator, enhancing both innate and adaptive immune responses. Research shows melatonin:

  • Stimulates the production of natural killer (NK) cells and T-helper cells
  • Has direct antiviral properties
  • Reduces inflammatory cytokine production in contexts of excessive immune activation

This immune dimension helps explain the strong correlation between sleep deprivation and infection susceptibility, since shortened nighttime hours also reduce melatonin production. The rhinovirus study showing 4.2× higher infection rates in people sleeping under 6 hours reflects the immune-consolidating function of sleep and the loss of melatonin’s immunomodulatory contribution.

Melatonin Across the Lifespan

Melatonin production is not constant across life:

  • Infancy and childhood: highest melatonin production; why children sleep so easily and deeply
  • Adolescence: puberty shifts melatonin onset approximately 2 hours later — the biological basis for “teenage night owls” is real and not laziness; school start times before 8:30 AM conflict with adolescent melatonin biology
  • Adulthood: stable production when circadian behaviors are maintained
  • Aging (50+): pineal gland begins to calcify; melatonin production decreases; sleep becomes lighter and more fragmented; this is one reason supplementation has more evidence for older adults

Why is the "teenage night owl" biological rather than behavioural?

Puberty shifts melatonin onset about two hours later. The adolescent is not physiologically ready for sleep at the adult time, which is why school start times before 8:30 AM run against the biology.

Why does supplementation have more evidential support in older adults?

Because the deficit is real there. The pineal gland calcifies with age and natural production genuinely declines, where in a younger person late melatonin is usually a behavioural timing problem instead.

Behavioural priorities in the sources

Fix the causes before reaching for the supplement:

  1. Get morning sunlight within 30–40 minutes of waking to anchor the countdown clock
  2. Dim lights after dark; eliminate overhead bright lighting in evenings
  3. Use blue-light blockers or aggressive screen dimming after 8–9 PM
  4. Stop caffeine by 1–2 PM (caffeine suppresses melatonin)
  5. Manage evening cortisol (elevated cortisol suppresses melatonin at the wrong time)
  6. Maintain consistent sleep and wake times to allow the SCN to predict timing

The source places light, schedule, caffeine, and stress timing before supplementation. It does not justify a universal supplement dose on this page.
