Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy is a domain-specific belief about one’s capacity to execute the actions a task requires. The cited essays distinguish it from global self-worth and vague confidence. It influences choice, effort, and persistence, while actual skill, opportunity, task difficulty, and support still constrain the outcome.

The Construct

Albert Bandura introduced self-efficacy in 1977 as part of his social cognitive theory. He defined it as an individual’s belief in their capacity to execute the behaviors required to produce specific outcomes.

The key distinctions:

  • Self-efficacy is domain-specific: High self-efficacy for coding does not transfer to public speaking. The same person can have high efficacy in one domain and low efficacy in another.
  • Self-efficacy is not self-esteem: Self-esteem is a global evaluation of self-worth. Self-efficacy is a specific judgment about capability in a specific context.
  • Self-efficacy is not confidence: Confidence is a feeling-state; self-efficacy is a belief that drives behavioral choices, effort, and persistence.

The sources present self-efficacy as a recurring predictor across performance domains because belief affects whether capability is deployed long enough to matter. Prediction is not proof of one-way causation: prior mastery raises efficacy, and both can be shaped by the same environment.

How does self-efficacy differ from self-esteem?

Self-esteem is a global judgement of worth. Self-efficacy is a specific judgement about capability in a specific domain — high for coding and low for public speaking in the same person, without contradiction.

The Four Sources

Bandura identified four sources from which self-efficacy is built:

1. Mastery experiences (most powerful source): Success builds self-efficacy for that domain, while failure undermines it. The causal sequence can run in either direction: attempt → success → increased efficacy → more attempts → more success; or attempt → failure → decreased efficacy → avoidance → less practice → more failure.

Starting with achievable tasks, including trivial ones, builds the efficacy needed for progressive challenge. Beginning with excessive difficulty and repeated failure creates a failure history that destroys efficacy before it can develop.

Which of Bandura's four sources is the most powerful, and why?

Mastery experience. Actually succeeding builds efficacy for that domain directly, and the loop runs both ways — success invites attempts, failure invites avoidance, which removes the practice that would have produced success.

2. Vicarious learning: Observing credible models succeed at a task increases self-efficacy for that task. “If someone like me can do this, so can I.” The model’s perceived similarity matters. An expert who performs without visible effort may discourage the observer because the expert seems too different. Someone who was once at the observer’s level offers stronger vicarious evidence.

Why can watching an expert reduce rather than raise your self-efficacy?

Vicarious learning depends on perceived similarity. An expert performing effortlessly reads as a different kind of person; someone who was recently where you are reads as evidence about you.

3. Social persuasion: Encouragement from credible sources produces a temporary increase in self-efficacy. Actual mastery experience must follow for the effect to solidify, and counterpersuasion from other credible sources can undo it.

4. Physiological and emotional states: Physical and emotional states affect efficacy assessments. A person interprets anxiety, fatigue, and dysphoria as signals of low capability: “I don’t have the energy/composure for this.” Positive physiological states support efficacy assessments. Sleep, exercise, and other biological regulators therefore affect cognitive and behavioral performance as well as self-efficacy.

The Behavioral Consequences

Self-efficacy affects behavior through four mechanisms:

Choice of activities: A person avoids activities for which they have low self-efficacy and pursues those for which they have high efficacy. Low efficacy produces active avoidance: the person makes no attempt. Without intervention, early damage to self-efficacy in a domain becomes permanent because avoidance prevents disconfirming experience.

How does early self-efficacy damage become permanent without intervention?

Low efficacy produces avoidance, and avoidance removes any chance of the mastery experience that would revise it. The belief protects itself by preventing the attempt that could disconfirm it.

Effort and persistence: higher efficacy can support persistence after setbacks, while low efficacy can promote avoidance. The authors’ mechanism is that belief changes how long capability is deployed, not that belief replaces capability or makes persistence wise when feedback says the strategy is failing.

Thought patterns: A person with low efficacy engages in rumination about inadequacies, attends selectively to failure, and attributes failures to personal inadequacy. A person with high efficacy focuses on strategy (“what do I need to do differently?”) rather than capability (“am I capable of this?”).

Resilience after setbacks: High-efficacy individuals attribute setbacks to insufficient effort or strategy, which is changeable. Low-efficacy individuals attribute setbacks to insufficient ability, which feels fixed. The attribution pattern directly determines whether the person tries again.

What separates high and low self-efficacy in how a setback is read?

The attribution. High efficacy attributes the setback to effort or strategy, which can be changed; low efficacy attributes it to ability, which feels fixed — and that reading decides whether there is a next attempt.

Building Self-Efficacy Deliberately

Bandura’s four sources support four methods:

Design mastery experiences: Break the domain into achievable subgoals, secure early wins, and increase the challenge over time. A person who has never exercised begins with something achievable, such as one ten-minute walk, rather than a marathon. The walk provides a mastery experience regardless of its scale.

Choose models strategically: Seek mentors, content creators, and examples of people who were recently where you are instead of people operating at an unattainable level. The model’s arc (“I started here, now I’m here”) builds more efficacy than their current performance alone.

Control physiological state: Sleep, exercise, nutrition, and breathing all affect efficacy assessments. The person who makes capability assessments from a depleted, anxious, or exhausted state is underestimating themselves. Build self-efficacy architecture when regulated.

Why should capability judgements not be made while exhausted?

Physiological and emotional state is one of the four inputs to the efficacy assessment. Fatigue and anxiety get read as evidence of incapability, so a depleted person systematically underestimates themselves.

Manage social persuasion inputs: Reduce exposure to sources that communicate low capability (“you can’t do this,” “people like us don’t succeed at this”) and seek credible sources that communicate high capability. Apply the same filter to the internal voice.
