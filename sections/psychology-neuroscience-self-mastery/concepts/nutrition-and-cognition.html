Nutrition and Cognition

What you eat is what your brain is made of and what your brain runs on. Most modern diets are optimized for caloric density and palatability, not for cognitive function or neurological health.

The Brain’s Nutritional Requirements

The brain is 2% of body weight but consumes approximately 20% of daily caloric intake. It is metabolically expensive and structurally dependent on dietary inputs:

  • Approximately 60% of the brain is fat — specifically, predominantly DHA (docosahexaenoic acid, an omega-3 fatty acid). The brain literally builds its structure from what you eat.
  • Glucose is the primary fuel, though ketones (from fat metabolism) can substitute and may offer neuroprotective effects
  • Neurotransmitter synthesis requires amino acid precursors: serotonin from tryptophan, dopamine from tyrosine and phenylalanine, GABA and glutamate from glutamine

The implication: dietary choices are not merely about energy — they are directly shaping the physical substrate of thought, mood, memory, and motivation.

Why is diet structural for the brain and not only fuel?

Roughly 60% of the brain is fat, predominantly DHA, and neurotransmitters are built from dietary amino acid precursors. What you eat becomes the substrate, not just the energy running through it.

The Omega-3 Priority

Omega-3 fatty acids — particularly DHA and EPA — are among the most evidence-supported nutritional interventions for brain health.

DHA is the primary structural fatty acid of the brain. Low DHA intake is associated with impaired cognitive function, increased depression risk, and reduced neurogenesis. The brain cannot synthesize sufficient DHA from precursors; it must come from diet (or supplementation).

Best sources: fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring) — 2-3 servings per week provides adequate DHA for most people. Algae-based DHA supplements provide the same source as fish (fish accumulate DHA by eating algae).

Why must DHA come from the diet?

The brain cannot synthesise enough of it from precursors. It is the primary structural fatty acid of the brain, and low intake tracks with impaired cognition, higher depression risk and reduced neurogenesis.

EPA from omega-3s has anti-inflammatory effects that protect neural tissue. Chronic neuroinflammation (associated with high omega-6/omega-3 ratios common in Western diets) impairs cognition and is implicated in depression and neurodegenerative disease.

Structure, Signalling, and Energy

what-neuroscientists-eat separates nutritional support into three jobs that
are easy to collapse into the vague category of “brain fuel”:

  • Structure: EPA, DHA, and phosphatidylserine contribute to the phospholipid
    membranes surrounding neurons. Ixcarus treats membrane integrity as a condition
    for signal transmission and resistance to damage.
  • Signalling: dietary choline, concentrated in egg yolks, is the precursor for
    acetylcholine. The work connects adequate acetylcholine with sustained attention
    and retention, and presents alpha-GPC as a more concentrated choline source.
  • Energy: firing neurons spend ATP. Creatine acts as a rapid regeneration
    buffer, which the work links to working memory and processing speed under
    cognitively demanding conditions.

This framework distinguishes a food’s role from its reputation. Fatty fish, eggs,
and protein matter because they supply particular substrates; supplements are
used to close a named gap rather than to create a general feeling of optimization.

The Gut-Brain Axis

The gut contains approximately 100 million neurons — more than the spinal cord. The gut microbiome (the bacterial ecosystem in the digestive tract) communicates bidirectionally with the brain via the vagus nerve, immune signaling, and neurotransmitter production.

Approximately 90% of serotonin is produced in the gut. The microbiome produces precursors for GABA, dopamine metabolites, and other neuroactive compounds. A dysbiotic microbiome (imbalanced bacterial populations, common with high-sugar, low-fiber, high-processed-food diets) directly impairs mood, stress response, and cognitive function.

Why does gut health show up as mood?

About 90% of serotonin is produced in the gut, and the microbiome also makes precursors for GABA and dopamine metabolites — communicating with the brain via the vagus nerve and immune signalling.

Microbiome support: dietary fiber (feeds beneficial bacteria), fermented foods (direct probiotic sources — yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut), diverse plant intake (different fiber sources feed different bacterial populations), reduced ultra-processed foods and refined sugar (feed dysbiotic bacteria).

Food preference is a learned loop

The gut-brain connection also influences what a person wants to eat. In
what-neuroscientists-eat, preference emerges from three signals: taste on the
tongue, subconscious nutrient sensing by gut neuropod cells, and learned
associations between a flavour and the metabolic result that follows it. Dopamine
reinforces foods whose sensed or predicted consequences supply value.

That makes preference trainable rather than fixed. Repeatedly pairing a less
appealing food with an already rewarding meal can transfer the prediction of
reward to the new taste. The inverse also matters: pairing sweetness with glucose
can teach the body to produce anticipatory dopamine and insulin when the sweet
taste later arrives without calories. The work therefore treats a craving as a
conditioned prediction within the Dopaminergic-System, not merely a verdict
issued by taste buds.

Expectation adds a final feedback path. Ixcarus cites an identical-milkshake
experiment in which different “indulgent” and “healthy” labels produced different
metabolic responses. In the work’s model, beliefs about food can participate in
the physiology of eating alongside nutrients and learned reward.

Fasting and Cognitive Function

Intermittent fasting and prolonged fasting produce several cognitive effects:

Metabolic switch: After approximately 12-18 hours of fasting, the liver depletes its glycogen stores and begins producing ketones from fat. The brain shifts from glucose to ketone metabolism. Many people report improved mental clarity in ketosis — possibly because ketone metabolism produces fewer reactive oxygen species than glucose metabolism, or because the mild stress of fasting triggers BDNF release.

Autophagy: Extended fasting (18-24+ hours) triggers autophagy — the cellular cleanup process that removes damaged proteins and organelles. Neuronal autophagy may be neuroprotective, clearing the cellular debris associated with aging and neurodegeneration.

What is the metabolic switch during a fast, and when does it arrive?

After roughly 12–18 hours the liver’s glycogen runs down and it begins producing ketones from fat. The brain shifts from glucose to ketone metabolism, which many people register as clarity.

BDNF elevation: Fasting is one of the documented stimuli for BDNF production. The evolutionary logic: in the ancestral environment, food scarcity was accompanied by the necessity for enhanced cognitive performance for finding food. The brain was designed to work well when hungry.

What is the evolutionary logic for fasting raising BDNF?

Scarcity was exactly when cognition mattered most — finding food demanded performance. The brain was built to work well hungry, so the fasted state reads as a cue to sharpen rather than to conserve.

Practical application: The simplest implementation — a 16:8 pattern (8-hour eating window, 16-hour fast) — captures many of the fasting benefits without requiring extended fasting.

Neuroprotective Foods

Foods consistently associated with brain health in research:

Berries: Anthocyanins (the compounds producing blue/purple/red color) cross the blood-brain barrier and have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in neural tissue. Blueberries specifically show cognitive benefits in clinical studies.

Leafy greens: Lutein, folate, phylloquinone (vitamin K), and α-tocopherol — all associated with slower cognitive decline. Highest-impact dietary category in observational brain health studies.

Nuts and seeds: Vitamin E (major antioxidant protecting neural membranes from oxidative damage), healthy fats, magnesium. Walnuts specifically have the highest omega-3 content of any nut.

Dark chocolate (>70% cacao): Flavonoids that increase cerebral blood flow, improve endothelial function, and have antioxidant effects. Among the few sweet foods with genuine cognitive benefit.

Coffee and tea: Caffeine’s acute cognitive effects are well-documented. Coffee also contains polyphenols with anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects beyond caffeine. Green tea’s L-theanine produces calm alertness through GABA-modulatory effects.

What to Reduce or Avoid

Ultra-processed foods and refined sugar: Spike blood glucose rapidly, producing insulin surges followed by glucose crashes — creating cycles of cognitive impairment. Chronic high-sugar diets impair hippocampal function and promote neuroinflammation.

Trans fats (partially hydrogenated oils): Associated with impaired memory and increased dementia risk.

Excessive alcohol: Neurotoxic, suppresses REM sleep (impairing memory consolidation), disrupts gut microbiome, depletes B vitamins required for neurological function.
