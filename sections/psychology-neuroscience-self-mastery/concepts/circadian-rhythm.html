Circadian Rhythm

Circadian rhythms are approximately daily patterns coordinated by central and peripheral clocks. The cited essays emphasise retinal light as the strongest timing cue for the central clock, with activity, food, and temperature also contributing. They connect misalignment to sleep and hormonal timing; this page does not treat every symptom as proof of a “broken clock.”

The Biological Clock

The word “circadian” derives from the Latin circa diem — approximately one day. Your body does not simply respond to light and darkness as external cues; it anticipates them. Internal molecular clocks operate in virtually every cell of the body, coordinated by a master pacemaker in the brain: the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), a cluster of roughly 20,000 neurons in the hypothalamus sitting directly above the optic chiasm — anatomically positioned to receive light information from the retina.

The SCN does not keep time passively. It actively synchronizes peripheral clocks throughout the body — in the liver, the gut, the immune system, the adrenal glands — to maintain physiological coordination across systems. When the master clock and peripheral clocks fall out of alignment (a state called circadian misalignment), every downstream system suffers simultaneously.

Why does circadian misalignment damage every system at once rather than one?

Because the SCN is not a clock you read — it actively synchronises peripheral clocks in the liver, gut, immune system and adrenals. When the master falls out of step, everything it was coordinating desynchronises together.

Light as the Primary Zeitgeber

Zeitgeber is the German term for “time-giver” — the external signal that entrains biological clocks. Light is the most powerful zeitgeber the human circadian system has.

What is a zeitgeber, and which is the strongest one humans have?

A “time-giver” — an external signal that entrains a biological clock. Light is by far the most powerful one the human circadian system responds to.

Morning light (specifically short-wavelength blue and UV light) strikes specialized photoreceptors in the retina called intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs). These cells project directly to the SCN and trigger a cascade:

  1. SCN receives the “daytime” signal
  2. This anchors the cortisol awakening response — triggering the body to ramp cortisol to its daily peak within the first hour of waking
  3. The SCN begins counting down to the evening melatonin release (~12–16 hours later)
  4. Body temperature regulation, digestion timing, immune function, and dozens of other rhythms synchronize against this morning anchor

Getting outside within 30–40 minutes of waking — even on overcast days — delivers significantly more photons to the retina than any indoor light source. Outdoor light on a cloudy day typically measures 10,000–20,000 lux; indoor lighting is generally 100–500 lux. The SCN calibration signal requires this intensity. Indoor light is simply insufficient.

Evening light is the inverse problem. The same short-wavelength light that activates the circadian system in the morning actively suppresses melatonin release when encountered at night. Every LED screen, bright overhead light, and blue-spectrum bulb encountered after dark is telling your circadian system that it is still midday. Melatonin onset is delayed by 1–3 hours in people who use screens in the 2 hours before sleep. This delay is not trivial — it compresses sleep duration and fragments the architecture of the night.

The Cortisol-Melatonin Axis

The circadian rhythm is, in practical terms, an oscillating relationship between two primary hormones:

Cortisol peaks in the first hour after waking — the Cortisol Awakening Response (CAR). This is the body’s neurological ignition: it mobilizes glucose, activates alertness, and primes the immune system for the day ahead. The morning light signal can amplify cortisol by up to 50%. After the morning peak, cortisol gradually declines throughout the day.

Melatonin rises in the evening as cortisol falls and light diminishes. The SCN’s countdown triggered by morning light determines when the pineal gland begins melatonin secretion — typically 12–16 hours after the morning light exposure. Melatonin does not cause sleep; it signals the body’s interpretation that it is nighttime, creating the hormonal environment permissive to sleep onset.

These two hormones are rhythmically oppositional. When cortisol is correctly high in the morning and low by evening, and melatonin rises undisturbed after dark, the circadian architecture is intact. When evening cortisol is elevated (from late light exposure, stress, or late caffeine) and morning cortisol is blunted, the feedback loop breaks down — producing the hallmark of burnout: wired at night, exhausted in the morning.

How does morning light determine when you become sleepy 14 hours later?

The morning signal starts the SCN’s countdown to melatonin release, typically 12–16 hours out. The evening sleepiness was scheduled that morning, which is why skipping the light delays it.

Social Jetlag and Circadian Disruption

Your circadian rhythm does not care about weekday/weekend distinctions. It wants consistency. When sleep timing shifts on weekends — sleeping in Saturday and Sunday, going to bed later Friday night — the SCN experiences what researchers call social jetlag: the internal clock is forced to misalign with behavior.

The downstream consequences are similar to actual transmeridian travel:

  • Sunday night insomnia (the internal clock has been shifted toward a later schedule and is not ready for sleep at the usual time)
  • Monday morning grogginess (cortisol awakening response is misaligned from the actual wake time)
  • Chronic mild circadian misalignment throughout the week

Maintaining consistent sleep and wake times including on days off is one of the highest-leverage single interventions for circadian health. The SCN builds a predictive rhythm — regular timing allows it to pre-position hormones, body temperature, and alertness before you even act.

What is social jetlag?

The misalignment produced by shifting sleep timing at weekends. The internal clock is pushed later without any travel, which is why Sunday insomnia and Monday grogginess follow the same pattern as crossing time zones.

Temperature and the Circadian Rhythm

Core body temperature is both a product and a driver of circadian rhythm. Body temperature follows a predictable 24-hour oscillation:

  • Evening temperature drop: core body temperature must fall approximately 1–2°C to initiate sleep onset. The body achieves this by vasodilating peripheral blood vessels — drawing heat toward the surface of the skin and away from core organs. This is why a warm bath before bed paradoxically aids sleep: it accelerates this surface-blood-flow mechanism, drawing heat to the skin so the core cools more rapidly when you get out.
  • Nighttime nadir: lowest core temperature occurs in the early morning hours (2–4 AM), coinciding with the deepest slow-wave sleep
  • Morning temperature rise: rising temperature is part of the cortisol awakening mechanism; exercise in the morning accelerates this and further entrains the circadian clock

By what mechanism does the body drop its core temperature for sleep?

Peripheral vasodilation — blood is drawn toward the skin, carrying heat away from the core organs. The 1–2°C fall that initiates sleep is achieved by radiating heat outward, not by producing less.

Room temperature profoundly affects sleep architecture. The optimal sleep environment temperature for most adults is 17–19°C (62–66°F). Rooms warmer than 20°C measurably reduce both slow-wave sleep duration and overall sleep quality.

Circadian Disruption: Downstream Effects

When the circadian rhythm is chronically disrupted — by shift work, late-night light exposure, irregular schedules, or chronic stress — the effects compound across every system:

  • Cognitive function: disrupted circadian rhythm impairs prefrontal cortex function, working memory, and attention even when total sleep time appears adequate
  • Metabolic health: circadian misalignment decouples insulin secretion timing from glucose intake, predisposing to insulin resistance regardless of diet quality
  • Immune function: immune cell activity is circadian-regulated; disruption impairs both innate and adaptive immune responses
  • Hormonal production: testosterone, growth hormone, thyroid hormones, and reproductive hormones all follow circadian patterns; disruption reduces peak production
  • Cancer risk: epidemiological data shows shift workers (chronically disrupted circadian rhythm) have elevated rates of certain cancers — the World Health Organization classifies night shift work as a probable carcinogen

How does circadian misalignment produce insulin resistance independently of diet?

It decouples the timing of insulin secretion from the timing of glucose intake. The same food arrives when the body is not hormonally prepared to handle it.

Circadian anchors proposed by the sources

Based on the authors’ framework, these are general anchors rather than an individualized prescription. Exact light duration, caffeine timing, temperature, and exercise recommendations depend on schedule, latitude, health, and response:

Morning:

  • Get outdoor light exposure within 30–40 minutes of waking (5–10 minutes minimum; no sunglasses)
  • Use a 10,000 lux light box if outdoor exposure is impossible (winter, early schedules)
  • Hydrate immediately — even mild dehydration blunts the cortisol awakening response
  • Exercise within the first 3 hours: physical activity entrains the circadian clock and amplifies the morning cortisol peak

Evening:

  • Dim overhead lighting after sunset; switch to warm amber or red-spectrum bulbs
  • Use blue-light-blocking glasses or aggressive night mode settings on screens after 8–9 PM
  • Stop caffeine by 1–2 PM (caffeine’s half-life is 5–6 hours; quarter-life 10–12 hours)
  • Keep bedroom temperature at 17–19°C
  • Maintain consistent sleep and wake times within ±30 minutes, including weekends
