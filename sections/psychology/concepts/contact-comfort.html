Contact Comfort

Contact comfort is the soothing an infant gets from soft bodily contact, which Harry Harlow found to be the main basis of the infant–mother tie, ahead of feeding.

In short

  • Infant rhesus monkeys given a cloth mother and a wire mother spent their time on the cloth one, whichever of the two gave milk.
  • Frightened infants ran to the cloth mother, and with her present they explored a strange room; without her they froze and cried.
  • The preference survived months of separation without waning, unlike a learned secondary reinforcer, which extinguishes.
  • So the secondary-drive account, in which love is learned from hunger reduction, does not explain attachment.

The experiment

Two heated surrogates of the same shape: one wire mesh sheathed in sponge rubber and terry cloth, one bare wire. Four infants were fed from the cloth mother and four from the wire mother. Over 165 days the wire-fed infants spent less and less time on the mother that fed them and more on the cloth mother that did not. Infants raised on one surrogate alone, cloth or wire, drank and grew equally well, so the difference was not nutritional.

The clue came by accident: lab-reared infants clung to the gauze pads on their cage floors and threw tantrums when the pads were changed. A newborn on bare mesh barely survived its first five days; on a terry-cloth cone it thrived.

Worked example: the strange room

Placed in a 6-foot room full of unfamiliar objects with the cloth mother present, an infant clutched her, then ventured out, explored, and came back between forays. With her absent the same infant crouched, froze and cried. A cloth mother behind clear Plexiglas still calmed them, though less than one they could touch. The mother works as a haven in fear and a base for exploring, two functions feeding cannot account for.

Limits

Monkeys first given surrogates at 250 days panicked for 12 to 48 hours before spending about nine hours a day on the cloth mother, about half the time of those reared with her; Harlow notes age is confounded with that difference. He lists rocking, postural support, sound and the face as other variables still to test. Watson had held that love is innate and elicited by touch, which Harlow’s data fit better than the derived-drive view.
