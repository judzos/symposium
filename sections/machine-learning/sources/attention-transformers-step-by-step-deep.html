Attention in transformers, step-by-step | Deep Learning Chapter 6

Attention is derived as a question-and-answer market — each token’s query asks something, each token’s key advertises an answer, and the value vector is the actual edit made to the asker’s embedding — and Sanderson ends by arguing the mechanism’s success owes less to any behaviour it enables than to the fact that it parallelises.

Key Concepts

  • The problem in three phrases: “American shrew mole,” “One mole of carbon dioxide,” “Take a biopsy of the mole.” After embedding, all three vectors for mole are identical, because the embedding is “effectively a lookup table with no reference to the context.” An attention block’s job is to compute what to add to that generic vector to move it toward one of several distinct meaning-directions.
  • Updates compose over distance: tower is a generic tall-noun direction; preceded by Eiffel it should move toward Paris, France and iron; preceded also by miniature it should stop correlating with large tall things at all. The extreme case: feed in a mystery novel ending “Therefore the murderer was…” and the final vector, which began life embedding was, must have absorbed everything in the context window relevant to the answer.
  • Query and key through a hypothetical example: for “A fluffy blue creature roamed the verdant forest,” imagine each noun asking “are there any adjectives sitting in front of me?” W_Q maps each embedding to a lower-dimensional query; W_K maps each to a key in the same small space, thought of as potential answers. Sanderson warns that the adjective-noun story is one behaviour the matrices could implement, not what they do; asked what W_Q does to non-nouns, he answers “Who knows.”
  • The attention pattern is a softmax over dot products: key·query measures alignment; large positive means fluffy attends to creature, while the against creature gives something small or negative. Raw scores run −∞ to ∞, so softmax is applied column by column to make each column a distribution.
  • The paper’s formula, reconciled with the diagrams: softmax(KᵀQ/√d_k)·V. Sanderson notes the Attention is All You Need convention differs: their K and Q hold vectors in rows, and they write QKᵗ, so their diagram is flipped diagonally from his. The √d_k denominator is for numerical stability.
  • Masking follows from an efficiency trick in training: the model predicts the next token after every subsequence simultaneously, so one training example acts as many. Simultaneous prediction requires blocking later tokens from influencing earlier ones and giving away the answer. Those entries are set to negative infinity before softmax, so they become zero while the columns stay normalised. Not all attention variants mask; GPT always does.
  • The attention pattern is the square of the context size: one row and one column per token. “This is why context size could act as a significant limitation for large language models and why scaling it up is nontrivial.”
  • The value matrix answers a different question: “if this word is relevant to adjusting the meaning of another word, what specific adjustments should be made?” Value vectors live in the full embedding space; weight them by the attention-pattern column, sum, and you get ΔE⃗, added to the original embedding to yield the refined E⃗′.
  • The value map uses a low-rank factorization: a square 12,288×12,288 value matrix would cost 150,994,944 parameters, far more than the key and query matrices at 12,288×128 = 1,572,864 each. In practice, Value↓ reduces the dimension to 128 and Value↑ restores it, constraining the overall map to a low-rank transformation and making all four matrices the same size: about 6.3 million parameters per head.
  • Multi-head, counted: GPT-3 runs 96 attention heads per block, each with its own key, query and value maps, producing 96 distinct attention patterns and 96 proposed ΔE⃗ per position, all summed and added to that position’s embedding. About 600 million parameters per multi-headed attention block; across 96 layers, just under 58 billion parameters, roughly a third of GPT-3’s 175 billion. “Even though attention gets all of the attention, the majority of parameters come from the blocks sitting in between these steps.”
  • The naming detail that trips people reading papers: in practice all the Value↑ matrices across heads are stapled into one giant output matrix belonging to the whole multi-headed block, and “the value matrix for a given attention head” usually means only the Value↓ projection.
  • Self- vs. cross-attention: everything above is self-attention. Cross-attention is nearly identical but the key and query maps act on different datasets (e.g. two languages in translation), and there is typically no masking, since there is no notion of later tokens affecting earlier ones.
  • Sanderson’s closing claim about why attention won: “A big part of the story for the success of the attention mechanism is not so much any specific kind of behavior that it enables, but the fact that it’s extremely parallelizable.” Given that scale alone has produced qualitative gains, parallelisable architectures have a structural advantage.

“It is the job of an attention block to calculate what it needs to add to the generic embedding, as a function of its context, to move it to one of those specific directions.”
