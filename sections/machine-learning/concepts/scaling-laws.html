Scaling Laws

Scaling laws estimate how model loss changes as researchers add parameters, training data, or compute. scaling-laws-neural-language-models fits power laws across each resource and then joins them into allocation rules. llama-3-herd-models shows a later model team using the same method to choose a frontier-scale run.

The three-way constraint

Kaplan and colleagues measure cross-entropy loss against non-embedding parameter count N, dataset tokens D, and training compute C. Loss follows a power law in each variable while the other two remain large enough to avoid a bottleneck. Their experiments cover six orders of magnitude in model size and more than eight in adjusted compute, yet architecture shape has a small effect across the tested Transformer configurations.

A budget cannot fund any one axis in isolation. More parameters yield diminishing returns once data limits the run. More tokens yield diminishing returns once model capacity limits it. More compute helps only when researchers assign it to a model and training schedule that can use it.

Allocation on the Kaplan frontier

The 2020 fit sends most new compute into parameters. Its compute-efficient prescription scales model size as C^0.73, critical batch size as C^0.24, and serial update steps as C^0.03. A one-epoch dataset grows as C^0.27. Under this fit, the team gains throughput from larger batches and capacity from larger models while adding little serial training depth.

This allocation connects scaling to gradient-descent. Batch size controls the noise and parallelism of each gradient estimate; update count controls the serial path; parameter count sets the work per token. Kaplan and colleagues use the gradient noise scale to estimate a critical batch size. Above that point, larger batches buy fewer steps at a rising compute cost.

The same fit favors early stopping. A large model reaches a target loss with fewer tokens and updates than a small model. The authors estimate that a compute-efficient run should stop about 10 percent above that model’s converged loss instead of training a smaller model to convergence.

A later allocation

The Meta Llama Team builds IsoFLOPs curves from models between 40 million and 16 billion parameters. It fits optimal training tokens as C^0.53 and projects a 402-billion-parameter model trained on 16.55 trillion tokens at 3.8 × 10²⁵ FLOPs. The team selects 405 billion parameters because its fitted loss curve has a broad minimum near that point.

The team makes a different choice for the 8B and 70B Llama-3 models. It trains them past their compute-optimal allocation to improve quality at a fixed inference budget. Compute-optimal training answers one operating question: how to minimize training loss for a training budget. Deployment cost can justify another point on the curve.

Limits

Kaplan and colleagues give their exponents as measurements from WebText2, one tokenizer, and one model family. They report weak fits in the smallest-data regime and no theory that derives the laws. Their compute estimate also omits context-dependent terms that matter for long sequences. The Llama team refits the relation on its own data.

Power-law loss forecasts do not prove gains on downstream tasks. The Llama team addresses that gap by linking task negative log-likelihood to training FLOPs, then linking likelihood to accuracy with scaling runs and older models. pretraining-evaluation covers that forecast and the benchmark checks around it.
