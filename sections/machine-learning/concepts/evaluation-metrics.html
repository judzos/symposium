Evaluation Metrics

Every claim a machine learning system makes about itself is a claim about a metric. Since each metric encodes a decision about which errors matter, choosing one is a modelling decision rather than a reporting formality.

flowchart TD
  Q[What decision must the metric support] --> C[Classification errors]
  Q --> R[Regression fit]
  Q --> G[Generated text quality]
  Q --> K[Ranking quality]
  Q --> P[Component behaviour]
  P --> E[End-to-end outcome]

Why is choosing a metric a modelling decision rather than a reporting one?

Each metric encodes a judgement about which errors matter. Picking one has already decided what the system is allowed to get wrong.

The classical core

vip-cheatsheet-machine-learning-tips-tricks supplies the 2018 CS229 foundation the rest of this page extends. It names the error types (false negatives are Type II, false positives are Type I), tabulates accuracy/precision/recall/specificity/F₁ against their interpretations, and gives the ROC equivalences explicitly: TPR = recall = sensitivity, FPR = 1 − specificity. Its description of F₁ is exactly right and rarely stated so plainly — “a hybrid metric useful for unbalanced classes,” which is a claim about when to use it, not just what it is.

For regression it insists on the model-selection criteria that penalise parameter count — Mallow’s Cₚ, AIC, BIC, adjusted R². Fit alone is not evidence.

No ground truth is the hard case. vip-cheatsheet-unsupervised-learning states the problem it cannot solve: “it is often hard to assess the performance of a model since we don’t have the ground truth labels.” Its substitutes — the silhouette coefficient and the Calinski-Harabaz index — measure whether clusters are dense and well separated, which is a property of the partition, not of its correctness. The metric answers a question adjacent to the one being asked. That gap is the same one Reward-Model lives in.

What do clustering metrics like the silhouette coefficient actually measure?

Whether the clusters are dense and well separated — a property of the partition, not of its correctness. With no ground truth available, the metric answers a question adjacent to the one being asked.

Classification: the confusion matrix and its readings

Accuracy, precision, recall, F₁, sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value, ROC, AUROC, equal error rate, DET curves — all derived from the same four cells, differing in what they divide by.

amans-ai-journal-primers-evaluation-metrics devotes a section to precision/recall vs. sensitivity/specificity, which is right: they are the same matrix read by two communities with different denominators, and the confusion between them is where a large share of metric errors originate.

Why is precision/recall confused with sensitivity/specificity so often?

They are the same confusion matrix read by two communities using different denominators. The cells are identical; only what each figure is divided by differs.

The primer also treats when F₁ becomes misleading under negative-class imbalance — the case where a healthy-looking score is measuring the wrong thing entirely. Its medical-diagnosis worked example makes the underlying point concrete: a false negative and a false positive are not comparable events, and no single number can pretend they are.

What does the medical-diagnosis example establish about single-number scores?

That a false negative and a false positive are not comparable events. A missed diagnosis and a false alarm cost different things, and any single number has already assumed they cost the same.

Generative text: two instruments, two questions

  • Perplexity — how well the model predicts held-out data.
  • Burstiness — the diversity and variability of its output.

They answer different questions, which is the reason to carry both. A model can score well on perplexity while repeating itself endlessly; burstiness catches that and perplexity cannot.

What failure does perplexity miss that burstiness catches?

Endless repetition. A model can predict held-out data well while producing no variety at all, so a good perplexity score says nothing about the diversity of what it generates.

  • BLEU — precision-oriented n-gram overlap, built for translation.
  • ROUGE / ROUGE-L — recall-oriented, built for summarisation. More reference sentences always improve the estimate.
  • BERTScore — embedding similarity instead of n-gram overlap, so paraphrase stops being penalised as error.

Why is BLEU precision-oriented and ROUGE recall-oriented?

Because of the tasks they were built for. Translation asks whether what was produced is correct — precision. Summarisation asks whether what mattered was kept — recall.

What error does BERTScore stop punishing?

Paraphrase. Scoring by embedding similarity rather than n-gram overlap means a correct answer in different words is no longer counted as wrong.

Each domain has its own family: regression, object detection, generative images, GANs, speech, clustering, code generation, compression, recommender systems. The breadth is itself the argument — there is no single number.

The primer’s major families now have focused graph pages:

  • classification-error-tradeoffs — denominators, thresholds, and asymmetric errors
  • regression-loss-geometry — absolute, squared, relative, and variance-based summaries
  • reference-based-text-evaluation — lexical overlap versus semantic similarity
  • rank-aware-evaluation — relevance, position, normalisation, and beyond-accuracy goals

Measure the components, not just the system

amans-ai-journal-primers-retrieval-augmented insists on evaluating retrieval and generation separately. A RAG system that answers badly may have retrieved perfectly and generated poorly, or retrieved garbage and generated from it faithfully. The aggregate score cannot distinguish them, and the two failures call for opposite fixes.

When the metric is itself a model

rlhf-reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedbackrlhf pushes this to its limit. The Reward-Model is a metric — a learned one, fit to comparisons rather than derived from a formula — and it inherits every property of a metric plus some new ones:

  • Its ceiling is the agreement of the people who produced its data: roughly 73% inter-labeler agreement.
  • It is only valid where it has seen data, and the policy being trained actively searches for the regions where it is wrong. Hence the KL penalty pinning the policy near its starting point.
  • Human preference is not a single function. Huyen prefers the losing response in a published example and says so — the metric is averaging a disagreement, not approximating a fact.

What does a learned metric inherit that a formula-defined one does not?

A training distribution. It is only valid where it has seen data — and unlike a formula, an optimiser can be pointed at the regions where it is wrong.
